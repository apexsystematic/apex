The finding that explains everything else

The problem isn't the tools. It's that the work was never redesigned around them.

McKinsey's core argument is straightforward: most processes in professional services firms were designed for a world without AI. Plugging an AI tool into a process built in 2005 doesn't change the process. It just adds a faster step inside a slow system.

The firms seeing real gains aren't using better tools. They're doing something different structurally — collapsing handoffs, removing coordination layers, and building workflows that treat automation as part of the sequence rather than an add-on to it. That distinction is why 90% invest and fewer than 40% see results.

Where the gap opens up

The difference between automating a task and redesigning a workflow

The report includes case studies across industries where the same principle holds. In each one, the gain came not from the AI tool itself — but from restructuring what surrounded it.

Sales process
Before
Human reps handling every enquiry from first contact through qualification to booking.
After
AI agents handle qualification and scheduling. Human specialists spend their time negotiating and building relationships.
Result
No headcount reduction. Significant reallocation of where skilled time goes.
Customer queries
Before
Every inbound query routed to a human agent regardless of complexity.
After
Conversational AI handles routine queries end-to-end. Humans handle complex and emotionally sensitive issues.
Result
Average cost per query cut by around 50%. Human attention directed where it matters most.
Document production
Before
Skilled professionals drafting documents from scratch, then reviewing, then revising.
After
AI produces the first draft. Skilled professionals review, refine, and ensure compliance.
Result
Time on first drafts cut by nearly 60%. Error rate down by around 50%. Same people, different work.

The pattern is consistent across every example. The tool does the repeatable part. The person does the part that requires judgement. The workflow is built to keep it that way — not left to chance.

The small firm implication

Large organisations are redesigning their workflows. Small ones are still buying tools.

The firms in McKinsey's case studies are large. But the underlying problem — processes designed before AI existed, tools bolted on without changing the structure — is identical in a five-person law firm or a solo accountant's practice.

The difference is that a large organisation has a project team to identify the workflow gaps, scope the build, and manage the implementation. A small firm has whoever isn't billing that day.

This is precisely why most small professional services firms end up in the 90% that invested and the 60% that saw nothing back. Not because the tools don't work. Because the connections were never built.

The gains McKinsey documents aren't out of reach for small firms. They just require the same structural thinking — applied at a smaller scale, with a fixed price and no ongoing dependency.

The gap between those firms and firms that haven't acted is widening every month.

What to do with this

Where to start if your processes were built before AI existed

McKinsey's prescription for organisations of any size is the same: don't automate tasks in isolation. Map the full workflow first, identify where handoffs and coordination layers are adding friction, then build the automation around the restructured process.

1
The starting point
Identify the workflows that cost the most time
Not tools, not individual tasks — workflows. The sequence from client enquiry to onboarded client. The sequence from completed matter to issued invoice. The sequence from call notes to CRM record to follow-up email. Each of these involves multiple steps, multiple tools, and multiple handoffs. That's where the time is.
2
The build question
Which connections are missing?
Most small firms already use individual tools that could be connected. A form tool. A calendar. A CRM of some kind. Email. The question is which of those are linked — and which require a human to copy information from one to another, manually trigger the next step, or check whether the previous one completed. Every manual handoff in a workflow is recoverable time.
3
The structural shift
Build the workflow so it runs without you
This is the McKinsey condition for seeing results: the process has to be redesigned, not just augmented. An automation that requires daily oversight isn't a workflow redesign — it's a new task. The goal is a sequence that runs, flags exceptions, and leaves the professional's time for the work that requires them specifically.
